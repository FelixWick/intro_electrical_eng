\begin{exercise}[Instantaneous and Average Power]
If $v(t)=160\cos(50t)\,\text{V}$ and $i(t)=-33\sin(50t-30^\circ)\,\text{A}$, calculate the instantaneous power and the average power.
\end{exercise}

\ifshowsolutions
\begin{solution}
First rewrite the current using
\[
\sin x = \cos(x-90^\circ):
\]
\[
i(t)=-33\cos\!\big[(50t-30^\circ)-90^\circ\big] = -33\cos(50t-120^\circ).
\]

The instantaneous power is
\[
p(t) = v(t)i(t) =160\cos(50t)\,\big[-33\cos(50t-120^\circ)\big] = -5280\cos(50t)\cos(50t-120^\circ).
\]

Use the identity
\[
\cos A \cos B = \tfrac12\left[\cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B)\right].
\]

Let \(A = 50t\), \(B = 50t-120^\circ\):
\[
p(t) = -5280 \cdot \tfrac12\left[\cos\big(120^\circ\big)+\cos\big(100t-120^\circ\big)\right] = -2640\left[\cos 120^\circ + \cos(100t-120^\circ)\right].
\]

Since \(\cos 120^\circ = -\tfrac12\),
\[
p(t) = -2640\left[-\tfrac12 + \cos(100t -120^\circ)\right] = 1320 - 2640\cos(100t-120^\circ).
\]

Thus the **instantaneous power** is
\[
\boxed{p(t)=1320 + 2640\cos(100t+60^\circ)\ \text{W}}.
\]

The **average power** is the DC term:
\[
\boxed{\overline{P} = 1320\ \text{W}}.
\]
\end{solution}
\fi

% ----- Exercise 2 -----
\begin{exercise}[Maximum Average Power]
Calculate the value of $Z_L$ in the circuit below in order for $Z_L$ to receive maximum average power. What is the maximum average power received by $Z_L$?

\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/max_avg_power.png}
\end{exercise}

\ifshowsolutions
\begin{solution}
Idea:
For a network seen by a load \(Z_L\), the maximum average power is delivered to \(Z_L\) when
\[
Z_L = Z_{th}^*,
\]
where \(Z_{th}\) is the Thevenin equivalent impedance seen by the load (with independent sources turned off). The maximum average power is then
\[
P_{\max}=\frac{|V_{th}|^2}{8\,\Re\{Z_{th}\}},
\]
where \(V_{th}\) is the open-circuit (Thevenin) voltage across the load terminals.

Thevenin impedance \(Z_{th}\):
Turn off the independent current source (an ideal current source \(\to\) open circuit). Then the two series branch impedances between the left and right load terminals are
\[
Z_{\text{top}} = (-j10)+30 = 30 - j10,
\qquad
Z_{\text{bottom}} = 40 + j20.
\]
These two series branches are in parallel, so
\[
Z_{th} = Z_{\text{top}}\parallel Z_{\text{bottom}}
= \frac{(30-j10)(40+j20)}{(30-j10)+(40+j20)}.
\]
Compute numerator and denominator:
\[
(30-j10)(40+j20)=1400 + j200,\qquad (30-j10)+(40+j20)=70+j10.
\]
Thus
\[
Z_{th}=\frac{1400+j200}{70+j10}.
\]
Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate \(70-j10\):
\[
Z_{th}=\frac{(1400+j200)(70-j10)}{70^2+10^2}
=\frac{100000}{5000}=20\ \Omega.
\]
So the Thevenin impedance is purely real:
\[
\boxed{Z_{th}=20\ \Omega.}
\]

Therefore the load for conjugate matching is
\[
\boxed{Z_L^{\text{opt}}=Z_{th}^*=20\ \Omega\ (\text{purely real}).}
\]

Thevenin voltage \(V_{th}\) (open-circuit voltage across the load):
With the load open, the circuit reduces to two series branches from top node \(T\) to bottom node \(B\):
\[
\text{Left branch: }Z_{L\,\text{total}} = (-j10)+40 = 40-j10,
\qquad
\text{Right branch: }Z_{R\,\text{total}} = 30 + j20.
\]
The independent current source is \(I_s=5\angle 90^\circ\ \mathrm{A}=j5\ \mathrm{A}\) (upwards from bottom to top). The equivalent impedance between top and bottom is
\[
Z_{\mathrm{eq}} = (40-j10)\parallel(30+j20).
\]
(Compute numerically)
\[
Z_{\mathrm{eq}} = 20.6 + j4.2\ \Omega.
\]
Hence the voltage between top and bottom (taking bottom as reference) is
\[
V_T = I_s\,Z_{\mathrm{eq}} = j5(20.6+j4.2) = -21 + j103\ \text{V}.
\]

Call the midpoint voltages (open-circuit) \(V_L\) (left mid) and \(V_R\) (right mid) measured with respect to bottom:
\[
I_{\text{left}}=\frac{V_T}{40-j10},\quad
I_{\text{right}}=\frac{V_T}{30+j20}.
\]
The mid-node voltages (voltage across the lower element in each branch) are
\[
V_L = I_{\text{left}}\cdot 40,\qquad V_R = I_{\text{right}}\cdot (j20).
\]
Carrying out the complex arithmetic (shown below):
\[
V_L = -44 + j92\ \text{V},\qquad V_R = -54 + j22\ \text{V}.
\]
Thus the open-circuit (Thevenin) voltage across the load terminals (left minus right) is
\[
V_{th}=V_L - V_R = ( -44 + j92) - ( -54 + j22) = 10 + j70\ \text{V}.
\]
In polar form
\[
|V_{th}|=\sqrt{10^2+70^2}=\sqrt{5000}=70.7107\ \text{V},\qquad
\angle V_{th}=\arctan\!\frac{70}{10}\approx 81.87^\circ.
\]
So
\[
\boxed{V_{th}=10 + j70\ \text{V} = 70.71\angle 81.87^\circ\ \text{V}.}
\]

Maximum average power:
With \(Z_L=Z_{th}^*=20\ \Omega\) and \(\Re\{Z_{th}\}=20\,\Omega\),
\[
P_{\max}=\frac{|V_{th}|^2}{8\,\Re\{Z_{th}\}}
=\frac{(70.71)^2}{8\cdot 20}
=\frac{5000}{160}=31.25\ \text{W}.
\]
\end{solution}
\fi

% ----- Exercise 3 -----
\begin{exercise}[Reactive Power Compensation]
An AC motor with impedance $Z_L = 4.2 + j\,3.6\,\Omega$ is supplied by a 220-V, 60-Hz source.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
  \item Find pf, P, and Q.
  \item Determine the capacitor required to be connected in parallel with the motor so that the power factor is corrected to unity.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\ifshowsolutions
\begin{solution}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item Power factor, real power \(P\), and reactive power \(Q\):

The magnitude and angle of the load impedance are
\[
|Z_L|=\sqrt{R^2+X^2}=\sqrt{4.2^2+3.6^2}=\sqrt{30.6}=5.531726674\ \Omega,
\]
\[
\theta=\angle Z_L=\arctan\!\frac{X}{R}=\arctan\!\frac{3.6}{4.2}=0.708626272\ \text{rad}=40.61^\circ.
\]

The RMS current is
\[
I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}}{|Z_L|}=\frac{220}{5.531726674}=39.77058393\ \mathrm{A}.
\]

The power factor (PF) is the cosine of the impedance angle (lagging because \(X>0\)):
\[
\mathrm{pf}=\cos\theta = \cos(0.708626272) = 0.7592\quad\text{(lagging)}.
\]

Compute complex power using \(S=V_{\mathrm{rms}}^2/(R-jX)^*\) equivalently
\[
S=\frac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}^2}{Z_L^*}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}^2(R+jX)}{R^2+X^2}
\]
so the real and reactive powers are
\[
P=\frac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}^2 R}{R^2+X^2},\qquad
Q=\frac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}^2 X}{R^2+X^2}.
\]

Numerically,
\[
R^2+X^2 = 4.2^2+3.6^2 = 30.6,
\]
\[
P = \frac{220^2\cdot 4.2}{30.6} \approx 6.643\,\mathrm{kW},
\]
\[
Q = \frac{220^2\cdot 3.6}{30.6} \approx 5.695\,\mathrm{kVAR}.
\]

\item Capacitor required in parallel to correct PF to unity:

To correct the power factor to unity we must supply capacitive reactive power \(Q_C\) that cancels the motor's inductive reactive power \(Q\). For a capacitor connected in parallel across the same RMS voltage \(V_{\mathrm{rms}}\),
\[
|Q_C| = V_{\mathrm{rms}}^2 \,\omega C,\qquad \omega=2\pi f.
\]
We need \( |Q_C| = Q \), hence
\[
C=\frac{Q}{V_{\mathrm{rms}}^2\omega}.
\]

\[
\omega=2\pi f = 2\pi\cdot 60 = 120\pi\ \mathrm{rad/s}\approx 376.9911184\,\mathrm{rad/s}
\]

\[
C=\frac{5694.1176471}{220^2\cdot 376.9911184} = 312\,\mu\mathrm{F}
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{solution}
\fi

\begin{exercise}[RMS Values and Average Power]
The voltage across a load and the current through it are given by
$v(t)=20+60\cos(100t)\ \text{V},\qquad i(t)=1-0.5\sin(100t)\ \text{A}$.
Find:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
  \item the RMS values of the voltage and of the current
  \item the average power dissipated in the load
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\ifshowsolutions
\begin{solution}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item For a signal composed of a constant (DC) term plus a single sinusoid,
\[
x(t)=X_{\mathrm{DC}}+X_m\cos(\omega t+\phi),
\]
the mean square (time average of \(x^2(t)\)) over one period is
\[
\overline{x^2(t)}=X_{\mathrm{DC}}^2+\frac{X_m^2}{2},
\]
because the cross term between DC and the sinusoid averages to zero and the sinusoid contributes \(X_m^2/2\). Thus the RMS value is \(\,X_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{X_{\mathrm{DC}}^2+\dfrac{X_m^2}{2}}\,.\)

Apply this to the voltage:
\[
V_{\mathrm{DC}}=20,\qquad V_m=60
\]
Hence
\[
V_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{20^2+\frac{60^2}{2}} = \sqrt{400+\frac{3600}{2}} = \sqrt{400+1800} = \sqrt{2200} = 10\sqrt{22}\ \text{V}.
\]
Numerically,
\[
V_{\mathrm{rms}}\approx 10\sqrt{22}\approx 46.9\ \text{V}.
\]

Apply the same formula to the current. Write the current as
\[
i(t)=I_{\mathrm{DC}}+I_m\cos(100t+\phi_i).
\]
Here \(I_{\mathrm{DC}}=1\). The AC term is \(-0.5\sin(100t)\), which has amplitude \(I_m=0.5\). (The sign and phase do not affect the RMS magnitude.) Thus
\[
I_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{1^2+\frac{(0.5)^2}{2}} = \sqrt{1+\frac{0.25}{2}} = \sqrt{1+0.125} = \sqrt{1.125} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{8}} = \frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}} = \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{4}\ \text{A}.
\]
Numerically,
\[
I_{\mathrm{rms}}\approx \sqrt{1.125}\approx 1.061\ \text{A}.
\]

\item The instantaneous power is
\[
p(t)=v(t)i(t)=(20+60\cos 100t)\bigl(1-0.5\sin 100t\bigr).
\]
Expand:
\[
p(t) = 20 \cdot 1 + 20 \cdot (-0.5\sin100t) + 60\cos100t \cdot 1 +60\cos100t \cdot (-0.5\sin100t).
\]
So
\[
p(t) = 20 -10\sin100t +60\cos100t -30\cos100t\sin100t.
\]
The average power \(P\) is the time average of \(p(t)\) over one period. Every pure sinusoid (or product of sinusoids of the same frequency that reduces to a sinusoid at twice the frequency) has zero average. Thus the
time average of \(-10\sin100t\), of \(60\cos100t\), and of \(-30\cos100t\sin100t\) is zero. Therefore only the DC term remains:
\[
P = \overline{p(t)} = 20\ \text{W}.
\]

Alternatively, using the decomposition into DC and sinusoidal parts:
\[
P = V_{\mathrm{DC}}I_{\mathrm{DC}}+\frac{1}{2}V_mI_m\cos\varphi,
\]
where \(\varphi\) is the phase difference between the voltage and current sinusoids. Here \(V_{\mathrm{DC}}I_{\mathrm{DC}}=20\cdot 1=20\) and the sinusoidal part has
\[
V_m=60,\qquad I_m=0.5.
\]
Voltage sinusoid: \(60\cos(100t)\) (phase \(0\)). Current sinusoid: \(-0.5\sin(100t)=0.5\cos(100t+\tfrac{\pi}{2})\) (phase \(+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)). Thus \(\varphi=0-(+\tfrac{\pi}{2})=-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\cos\varphi=\cos(-\tfrac{\pi}{2})=0\). Hence the sinusoidal contribution is zero and
\[
P=20\ \text{W}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{solution}
\fi

\begin{exercise}[Complex Power Calculation]
For the entire circuit below, calculate:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
  \item the complex power
  \item the average power delivered by the source
  \item the reactive power
  \item the apparent power
  \item the power factor
\end{enumerate}

\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/complex_power.png}
\end{exercise}

\ifshowsolutions
\begin{solution}
\[
Z_1 = 10 - j5,\qquad Z_2 = 8 + j6.
\]

Parallel of the two branches:
\[
Z_P = \left(\frac{1}{Z_1}+\frac{1}{Z_2}\right)^{-1} = \Big(\frac{1}{10-j5}+\frac{1}{8+j6}\Big)^{-1} = 6.1538461538 + j\,0.7692307692\ \Omega.
\]

Total series impedance (including the 2\(\Omega\) resistor):
\[
Z_{T} = 2+Z_P = 8.1538461538 + j\,0.7692307692\ \Omega.
\]

Source current (phasor):
\[
I_s=\dfrac{V_s}{Z_T} =\dfrac{16\angle 45^\circ}{8.1538461538 + j\,0.7692307692} \approx 1.505034617 + j\,1.245545890\ \text{A}.
\]

\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
\item Complex power delivered by the source (using \(S=V_s I_s^*\)):
\[
S_s = V_s I_s^* = 31.12 + j\,2.932\ \text{VA}.
\]

\item Average (real) power delivered by source:
\[
P = \Re\{S_s\}=31.12\,\text{W}
\]

\item Reactive power:
\[
Q=\Im\{S_s\}=2.932\ \text{VAR}
\]

\item Apparent power:
\[
|S_s|=\sqrt{P^2+Q^2}=31.26\ \text{VA}.
\]

\item Power factor (scalar) and type:
\[
\text{pf}=\dfrac{P}{|S_s|}=\dfrac{31.12}{31.26}\approx 0.9956.
\]
Since \(Q>0\) the net load is inductive, so the power factor is \emph{lagging}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{solution}
\fi

% ----- Exercise 6 -----
\begin{exercise}[Series Resonance]
A series $RLC$ network has $R = 2\,\text{k}\Omega$, $L = 40\,\text{mH}$, and $C = 1\,\mu\text{F}$. Calculate the impedance at resonance and at one-fourth, one-half, twice, and four times the resonant frequency.
\end{exercise}

\ifshowsolutions
\begin{solution}
For s series RLC network with
\[
R=\SI{2}{\kilo\ohm},\qquad L=\SI{40}{\milli\henry},\qquad C=\SI{1}{\micro\farad}
\]
the series impedance is
\[
\underline{Z}(j\omega)=R+j\bigl(\omega L-\tfrac{1}{\omega C}\bigr).
\]

Resonance occurs when the net reactance is zero:
\[
\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.
\]
Substitute the values:
\[
\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(40\times10^{-3})(1\times10^{-6})}}=\SI{5000}{\radian\per\second}.
\]

For a given angular frequency \(\omega\),
\[
X_L=\omega L,\qquad X_C=\frac{1}{\omega C},\qquad X=X_L-X_C,
\]
\[
\underline{Z} = R + jX,\qquad |\underline{Z}| = \sqrt{R^2 + X^2},\qquad \varphi = \arctan\!\frac{X}{R}.
\]

We evaluate at \(\omega = k\omega_0\) for \(k=\tfrac14,\tfrac12,1,2,4\).

\bigskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
\toprule
$k$ & $\omega$ (rad/s) & $X_L$ (\si{\ohm}) & $X_C$ (\si{\ohm}) & $\underline{Z}$ (\si{k\ohm}) & $|\underline{Z}|$ (\si{k\ohm}) & $\varphi$ \\
\midrule
$0.25$ & $1250$ & $50$ & $800$ & $2 - j0.75$ & $2.136$ & $-20.6^\circ$ \\[4pt]
$0.5$ & $2500$ & $100$ & $400$ & $2 - j0.3$ & $2.022$ & $-8.5^\circ$ \\[4pt]
$1$ & $5000$ & $200$ & $200$ & $2$ & $2$ & $0^\circ$ \\[4pt]
$2$ & $10000$ & $400$ & $100$ & $2 + j0.3$ & $2.022$ & $+8.5^\circ$ \\[4pt]
$4$ & $20000$ & $800$ & $50$ & $2 + j0.75$ & $2.136$ & $+20.6^\circ$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\bigskip
\noindent Observations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item At resonance ($k=1$) the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel, leaving purely resistive impedance \(Z=R=\SI{2000}{\ohm}\).
  \item The network is capacitive ($X<0$) for frequencies below resonance and inductive ($X>0$) for frequencies above resonance.
  \item The magnitudes at pairs \(k=\tfrac14\) and \(k=4\) are equal (symmetry), and likewise for \(k=\tfrac12\) and \(k=2\).
\end{itemize}
\end{solution}
\fi

% ----- Exercise 7 -----
\begin{exercise}[Parallel Resonance]
Rework Exercise 6 if the elements are connected in parallel.
\end{exercise}

\ifshowsolutions
\begin{solution}
For the parallel connection the total admittance is
\[
\underline{Y}(j\omega) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{R}}_{G} + j\Bigl(\underbrace{\omega C}_{B_C} + \underbrace{\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\omega L}\Bigr)}_{B_L}\Bigr) = G + jB(\omega).
\]
The impedance is the reciprocal of the admittance:
\[
\underline{Z}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\underline{Y}(\omega)} = \frac{1}{G+jB} = \frac{G-jB}{G^2+B^2}.
\]
Its magnitude and phase are
\[
|\underline{Z}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{G^2+B^2}},\qquad \varphi = \operatorname{atan2}\bigl(\Im\{Z\},\Re\{Z\}\bigr) = \arctan\!\frac{-B}{G}.
\]

Parallel resonance (net susceptance zero) occurs at the same angular frequency as series resonance:
\[
B(\omega_0) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.
\]
With the given values
\[
\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(40\times10^{-3})(1\times10^{-6})}} = \SI{5000}{\radian\per\second}.
\]
Also
\[
G = \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{2000} = \SI{5e-4}{\siemens}.
\]

At resonance $B(\omega_0)=0$, so
\[
Y(\omega_0) = G \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad Z(\omega_0) = \frac{1}{G} = R = \SI{2000}{\ohm}.
\]

We compute for each ratio \(k\):
\[
\omega = k\omega_0,\qquad B = \omega C-\frac{1}{\omega L},\qquad \underline{Z} = \frac{G-jB}{G^2+B^2}.
\]

\bigskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
\toprule
$k$ & $\omega$ (rad/s) & $B_C$ (\si{m\ohm}) & $B_L$ (\si{m\ohm}) & $\underline{Z}$ (\si{\ohm}) & $|\underline{Z}|$ (\si{\ohm}) & $\varphi$ \\
\midrule
$0.25$ & $1250$ & $1.25$ & $20$ & $1.42 + j\,53.30$ & $53$ & $+88.5^\circ$ \\[4pt]
$0.5$ & $2500$ & $2.5$ & $10$ & $8.85 + j\,132.74$ & $133$ & $+86.2^\circ$ \\[4pt]
$1$ & $5000$ & $5$ & $5$ & $2000$ & $2000$ & $0^\circ$ \\[4pt]
$2$ & $10000$ & $10$ & $2.5$ & $8.85 - j\,132.74$ & $133$ & $-86.2^\circ$ \\[4pt]
$4$ & $20000$ & $20$ & $1.25$ & $1.42 - j\,53.30$ & $53$ & $-88.5^\circ$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\bigskip
\noindent Notes on the results:
\begin{itemize}
  \item At resonance the susceptances of the inductor and capacitor cancel and the parallel branch behaves as a pure conductance $G$, giving $Z(\omega_0)=R$.
  \item For frequencies below resonance ($k<1$) the net susceptance $B$ is negative (inductive behavior), producing an impedance with a large positive imaginary part (inductive reactance).
  \item For frequencies above resonance ($k>1$) $B$ is positive (capacitive behavior), producing impedance with a large negative imaginary part (capacitive reactance).
  \item The impedances occur in conjugate pairs for reciprocal frequency ratios (e.g. $k=0.25$ and $k=4$; $k=0.5$ and $k=2$).
\end{itemize}
\end{solution}
\fi
